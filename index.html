I work on genome annotation for neglected parasitic organisms, combining a background in genetics and computer science with methods from machine learning and comparative genomics.

My first master's degree, in genetics, focused on the computational side of a tumor-suppressor gene study: analyzing high-throughput sequencing data from a barcoded, CRISPR-based screen to track how the same gene behaved across thousands of tumors carrying different cancer-driving mutations. That project is where the context-dependence I now study in genome annotation first became concrete. A second master's degree, in computer science, deepened the analytical and engineering side of that work.

I am currently a PhD researcher at Temple University's Institute for Genomics and Evolutionary Medicine, working across the Pond Lab and the Escalante Lab. My dissertation focuses on the parasites that cause malaria, babesiosis, and toxoplasmosis — organisms whose genes are shaped unusually and whose annotation quality is rarely checked against a confirmed answer, yet matters directly for tracking and eventually treating the diseases they cause.

How do we know how much to trust a predicted gene when there is no verified answer key to check it against?

Every tool that finds genes in a genome — a decades-old statistical method or a modern AI model — has to guess where a gene starts, where it ends, and how its pieces fit together, and these tools tend to report that guess with more confidence than the evidence actually supports. They are also built and tested almost entirely on a handful of well-studied organisms, such as humans, mice, and crop plants. My research instead focuses on neglected apicomplexan parasites, whose compact and irregular gene structure causes standard annotation methods to fail quietly rather than visibly.

Uncertainty-Aware Genome Annotation

A framework that scores every predicted gene not with a single confidence number, but four: how much independent methods agree with each other, how well the prediction matches expressed RNA, how consistent it is with related species, and how confident the underlying model itself is. Splitting confidence this way means a low score can be traced to a specific, useful cause rather than reported as an unexplained warning.

Confidence decompositionEvidence benchmarking

Rethinking Gene-Finding for Unusual Genomes

An alternative approach to finding genes in the first place, adapting a technique originally built for locating events in video to locate genes in DNA. It is being evaluated specifically on organisms whose compact, irregular gene structure causes standard hidden-Markov-model-based methods to fail.

Non-HMM predictionNeglected pathogens

Longer term, the goal is a calibrated, well-documented sense of where a genome annotation can and cannot be trusted — a resource researchers on any under-studied organism can use immediately, independent of which prediction tool eventually wins out.
